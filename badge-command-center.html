Locomotive
The Daniel Nason — a 4-4-0 American Standard, NYP&B Railroad. This line opened the Shore Line in 1837, one of the earliest rail corridors in New England. The 4-4-0 was the workhorse of 19th century American railroading — elegant, reliable, and built for distance.
Historical Fact
Working Class families in 1930 earned between $500 and $1,000 annually. A parlor car ticket was an occasional indulgence — a Sunday ride, a special occasion. Something to talk about at the table.

Locomotive
NYNH&H C-2 Class 4-4-0 No. 1703 — built by the Rhode Island Locomotive Works in 1883. Rhode Island built, Rhode Island operated. Photographed arriving at Sockanosett around 1905. A local machine on a local line, connecting the Pawtuxet Valley to the broader New Haven network.

Middle Class households in 1930 earned between $1,000 and $2,500 a year. A sleeping car berth meant overnight travel in relative comfort — arriving rested, presentable, and ready. A small but meaningful distinction.

Newport & Narragansett Bay Railroad
Dining Car Regular

"Your usual table is ready."
Locomotive
The Aquidneck Spruce — originally built as a 54-seat coach for the Pennsylvania Railroad in 1946. Now operating as the Newport Dinner Train along the Narragansett Bay coastline. White tablecloths, soft lighting, the bay outside the window. Some railroad cars were built to move people. This one was built to slow them down.
Historical Fact
Upper Middle Class families in 1930 earned between $2,500 and $5,000 annually. Dining car service was expected at this level — not a treat, a standard. The table was set before they boarded.

Hartford Providence & Fishkill Railroad
Private Car Guest
Tap to flip
"The car is yours. We'll handle the rest."
Locomotive
HP&F engine "The Bee" — a named, documented locomotive photographed on the Hartford Providence & Fishkill line in the 19th century. Inbound from the Connecticut state line, The Bee worked every stop on the Rhode Island corridor: Greene → Washington → Summit → Anthony → Quidnick → Arctic → River Point → Olneyville → Dike Street → Providence Union Station. Mill towns, factory villages, working Rhode Island from end to end. The western section of this line — abandoned in 1968 — is now known locally as the Trestle Trail, a hiking and biking path through the Coventry and Greene corridor where the tracks once ran. The rails are gone. The geography remembers.
Historical Fact
Wealthy households in 1930 earned between $5,000 and $20,000 a year. A private car arrangement meant the railroad came to you — your schedule, your guests, your menu. The timetable was a suggestion.
⚠ CMD CTR ONLY · $5,000 lifetime spend · Wealthy
Narragansett Pier Railroad
Pullman Patron
Tap to flip
"They don't print your name on just anyone's car."
Locomotive
ALCO 2-6-0 Mogul No. 11 — ordered 1922, arrived 1923. The Narragansett Pier Railroad's primary steam locomotive in its final passenger era. A compact Mogul working an 8.5 mile run: West Kingston → Peace Dale → Wakefield → Narragansett Pier. Summer crowds, coastal air, the kind of ride people planned their seasons around. The tracks are gone now — the right of way lives on as the William C. O'Neill Bike Path, tracing the same route to the sea.
Historical Fact
Very Wealthy households in 1930 earned between $20,000 and $50,000 annually. Pullman service meant a porter who remembered your preferences, a berth made up before you asked, and a car that felt more like a private club than a train.
⚠ CMD CTR ONLY · $10,000 lifetime spend · Very Wealthy
New York New Haven & Hartford Railroad
Railroad Magnate
Tap to flip
"You don't ride the train. You own the tracks."
Locomotive
Class I-5 4-6-4 Hudson No. 1400 — semi-streamlined, Art Deco, late 1930s. The locomotive that hauled the Merchants Limited along the Shore Line. Built for speed and presence, the I-5 was the crown of the New Haven's passenger fleet. Behind it, the Industrial Trust Building rose over Providence — two minutes from Union Station, the tallest structure in Rhode Island, a monument to the same era. Both built at the peak. Both now waiting for their next chapter.
Historical Fact
Railroad Magnates in the 1930s didn't have salaries — they had empires. Stockholders, board members, the names on the stations. Their income was measured not in wages but in ownership. In the 1930s that meant controlling the movement of an entire region.
⚠ CMD CTR ONLY · $20,000 lifetime spend · Magnate Class · "If someone spends $20K they might as well be a stockholder."

In 1943 copper was diverted for the war effort so pennies were struck on zinc-coated steel for one year. A handful were accidentally struck on leftover copper planchets. Those error pennies are now worth $100,000 or more. Check your change.

Fun Fact
In 1937 at the Denver Mint, a pressman over-polished a die to remove surface marks and accidentally erased the buffalo's front right leg. Thousands of "three-legged" nickels were released before the error was caught. A circulated example is worth around $612 today. Uncirculated: $2,258–$5,221 or more.

Level 1
In Circulation
1916–1930 · 90% silver, 10% copper
Fun Fact
The original 1916 design featured Lady Liberty with one breast fully exposed. The public outcry was immediate — religious leaders called it obscene, citizens' groups lobbied Congress, and the design was revised in 1917 to cover her in a coat of chain mail. Only 52,000 of the original bare-breasted quarters were struck, making them extremely rare today.

Fun Fact
The Peace Dollar was commissioned in 1921 to commemorate the end of World War I — the first U.S. coin explicitly designed as a symbol of peace. Sculptor Anthony de Francisci used his wife Teresa as the model for Lady Liberty. She had recently immigrated from Italy and later said posing for the coin was the proudest moment of her life.
